The morning ferry left the harbour a few minutes after seven.
Gulls followed it out past the breakwater, hoping for crumbs.
On the upper deck, a man in a green coat was reading a map of the islands.
His daughter counted the lighthouses and found four before the fog rolled in.
The captain announced that the crossing would take about forty minutes.
Below deck, the small café sold tea, toast and slices of lemon cake.
A woman with a cello case asked whether the seats near the window were free.
Outside, the water changed from grey to a deep and quiet blue.
Somebody's radio was playing an old song about a train that never came.
By half past seven, the first island appeared as a low dark line.
Its harbour had a red crane, a row of white houses and one stubborn bell.
The ferry slowed, turned in a wide arc and nudged against the pier.
Ropes were thrown, caught and tied with a speed that looked effortless.
Passengers gathered their bags and shuffled toward the narrow gangway.
The girl waved at a dog that was barking at the waves from the quay.
Her father folded the map, although he would need it again in an hour.
The cellist stayed on board, because her concert was on the last island.
Fresh crates of apples and bread were carried on for the return trip.
At eight o'clock the bell rang twice, and the ferry pulled away again.
The fog had lifted, and the whole bay was bright with early sun.
